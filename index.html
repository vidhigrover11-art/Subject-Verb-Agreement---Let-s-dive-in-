What is subject–verb agreement?

🧍 The subject

The person, animal, place or thing the sentence is about—or the one doing the action.

The astronaut explores Mars.

Ask: Who or what is the sentence about?

🏃 The verb

The action word or being word that tells what the subject does or is.

The astronaut explores Mars.

Ask: What is the subject doing? What is it like?

The golden rule: The subject and the verb must match!

One robot works.
Singular subject → works
Three robots work.
Plural subject → work
🧠 The surprising trick: In the simple present tense, one person or thing often gets the verb with -s; more than one usually gets the verb without -s.
2

Meet the main rules

Rule 1 · One vs. many

A singular subject takes a singular verb. A plural subject takes a plural verb.

The comet glows at night.
The comets glow at night.

Rule 2 · He, she, it

In the simple present, use a verb ending in -s or -es with he, she, it, or one person's/thing's name.

Maya watches the stars.
They watch the stars.
Try it: go → goes · fix → fixes · study → studies

Rule 3 · I and you are special

Use the base verb with I and you, even when you mean one person.

I play chess. ✓
You play chess. ✓
She plays chess. ✓

Rule 4 · Two subjects joined by “and”

Two subjects usually make a plural subject, so use a plural verb.

Riya and Kabir are curious.
The cat and the dog chase the ball.
Remember: Riya + Kabir = they!

3

Five clever detective tricks

Find the real subject. Ask “Who or what is doing it?” Don't choose a noun just because it is closest to the verb.
Cover the extra words. “The basket of bright apples is heavy.” Cover the middle phrase: “The basket is heavy.” The subject is basket, not apples.
Swap the subject for a pronoun. “The children” → “they,” so say “The children are ready.” “The child” → “he/she,” so say “The child is ready.”
Watch for “each” and “every.” These usually point to one at a time, so use a singular verb: “Every planet has a path.”
Read it aloud. “The birds sings” sounds bumpy. Check the subject, then test the verb: “The birds sing.”

⚡ The “S Switch”

For most simple-present action verbs, the -s switches sides:

One bird → bird flies
Many birds → birds fly

Important: This shortcut is for the simple present tense. In the past tense, “walked” works with both “she” and “they.”

4

Tricky cases made easy

🔍 Don't let these sentences fool you

Words between the subject and verb

The box of crayons are on the table. → The box of crayons is on the table.

The subject is box. “Of crayons” only adds information.

Each, everyone, somebody, nobody

Everyone is invited. · Each student has a notebook. · Nobody knows the answer.

These words are treated as singular in standard school grammar.

Either…or / Neither…nor

The verb usually agrees with the subject closest to it:

Either the teacher or the students are presenting.

Either the students or the teacher is presenting.

There is / There are

Look at the noun that comes after the verb:

There is one mystery. · There are three mysteries.

Collective nouns: a team, class or family

When the group is acting as one unit, use a singular verb: The team is ready. · The class enjoys the experiment.

Modal verbs: can, should, must, will

After a modal verb, use the base form of the action verb:

She can swim. ✓ Not “She can swims.”

The robots must follow the rules.

Your pocket-sized recap

Remember these!

✓ One subject: the dog runs

✓ Many subjects: the dogs run

✓ Joined by “and”: usually plural

✓ Each / everyone: singular

✓ Ignore extra words between subject and verb.

The 3-second check

1. Find the subject.

2. Decide: one or more than one?

3. Choose the verb that matches.
